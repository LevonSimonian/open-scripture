Before you start

Four things that make it click

It's a library, not a book

Seventy-three separate documents, written by dozens of people across roughly 1,600 years, in three languages, later bound together. Each bar in the strip above is one book.

It isn't in time order

The books are grouped by type — history, poetry, prophecy, letters — so reading front to back means jumping around in time. That's the single biggest reason people get lost.

Two testaments, one hinge

"Testament" means agreement. The Old Testament is the story before Jesus, and is also the Jewish scriptures. The New Testament is Jesus and what happened next.

How to read a reference

References are addresses. Book name, chapter, then verse. Chapters and verses were added centuries after the writing, purely so people could find things.

John 3:16  →  book, chapter 3, verse 16

If your Bible has 66 books, not 73

Both are normal. This site follows the Catholic canon, which includes seven books Protestant Bibles leave out — Tobit, Judith, 1 and 2 Maccabees, Wisdom, Sirach and Baruch — and orders parts of the Old Testament differently. Everything else is identical, and all traditions share the same 27 New Testament books.

Look for the Deuterocanon tag on the shelf to see which seven those are. If a book sits at a different number in your Bible, that's why — the names always match.

Guide one · the story

The whole story,
in fifteen moves

Read it top to bottom in about fifteen minutes and you'll be able to follow the whole book — what happens, in what order, and why any of it connects. The strip that appears as you scroll shows which books cover each stretch.

01Before history

In the beginning

Genesis 1–11

God makes a world and calls it good, and makes people to look after it. They break trust with him and are put out of the garden. What follows goes badly fast: the first murder, a flood that spares one family in a boat, and a tower built to reach heaven that ends with humanity scattered across the earth.

These chapters aren't the plot — they're the setup. They establish a good world, a fracture running through it, and a problem that the remaining thirteen hundred chapters are trying to solve.

Adam & EveCain & AbelNoahBabel
Keep hold of

Something is broken between people and God. Everything after this is a repair attempt.

02c. 2000–1700 BC

One family, one promise

Genesis 12–50

God picks one elderly, childless man — Abraham — and promises him land, descendants beyond counting, and that through his family the whole world will be blessed. Abraham believes it, mostly, and the rest of Genesis follows three more generations trying to live inside that promise.

His grandson Jacob is renamed Israel, which becomes the name of the nation. Jacob's son Joseph is sold into slavery in Egypt by his jealous brothers, rises to run the country, and saves that same family from famine. They settle in Egypt. Genesis ends there.

Keep hold of

That one promise to Abraham is the thread. When later writers argue about what God is doing, they're arguing about this.

03c. 1450–1250 BC

Rescue from Egypt

Exodus · Leviticus · Numbers · Deuteronomy

Four hundred years later Abraham's descendants are slaves. Moses, a Hebrew raised in Pharaoh's household, is sent to demand their release. Ten plagues, an escape through a parted sea, and a nation walks out of Egypt in a night.

At Mount Sinai they're given a law — the Ten Commandments plus hundreds of rules for worship, justice and daily life — that defines them as God's people. Then they lose their nerve at the border of the promised land, spend forty years in the desert, and Moses dies within sight of it.

Heads up: Leviticus and much of Numbers are legal text. First-time readers skim them without guilt.

Keep hold of

The exodus is the defining rescue story. For the next thousand pages, when anyone says "remember what God did," this is what they mean.

04c. 1200–1050 BC

Taking the land

Joshua · Judges · Ruth

Under Joshua the nation crosses into Canaan and takes territory — a violent, contested section that readers wrestle with, and should. Then Joshua dies and there's no leader.

Judges runs a bleak loop, over and over: the people drift, an enemy oppresses them, they cry for help, and a "judge" — a rescuer or warlord, not a courtroom judge — pulls them out. Gideon, Deborah, Samson. Each cycle ends worse than the last. Ruth is a small, warm story of loyalty set in the same grim period.

JoshuaJerichoDeborahGideonSamsonRuth
Keep hold of

Leaderless doesn't work. By the end the people are demanding a king like everyone else has.

05c. 1050–930 BC

Israel gets a king

1–2 Samuel · 1 Kings 1–11 · 1–2 Chronicles

Saul is crowned first and unravels. Then David — shepherd boy, giant-killer, songwriter, guerrilla fighter — becomes the great king, unites the tribes and takes Jerusalem. He's also the man who sleeps with another man's wife and has the husband killed, and the book doesn't soften it.

God makes David a promise: one of his descendants will rule a kingdom that never ends. Hold onto that. His son Solomon builds the temple in Jerusalem, becomes famous for wisdom and wealth, and then drifts badly.

Keep hold of

"Messiah" means anointed king. From here on, it means a king from David's family line. That's the job description Jesus later gets measured against.

06930–586 BC

The kingdom splits

1–2 Kings · 2 Chronicles · Isaiah · Jeremiah · Hosea · Amos · Micah and more

After Solomon the nation tears in two: Israel in the north, Judah in the south with Jerusalem. Mostly bad kings on both sides, for three centuries.

God sends prophets — Elijah, Elisha, Isaiah, Jeremiah, Amos, Hosea — with the same two messages: you've abandoned the agreement, and you're crushing poor people, and it will cost you. Assyria destroys the northern kingdom in 722 BC. Judah gets another 140 years.

ElijahElishaIsaiahJeremiahJezebelAssyria
Keep hold of

Prophets are closer to whistleblowers than fortune-tellers. But mixed into the warnings are promises of a rescuer to come — and those get quoted constantly in the New Testament.

07586–538 BC

Exile

2 Kings 25 · Lamentations · Ezekiel · Daniel

Babylon burns Jerusalem, levels the temple and deports the survivors. This is the floor of the Old Testament: no land, no king, no temple — every visible sign of the promise gone at once.

Daniel survives as a civil servant in a foreign court. Ezekiel sees strange, blazing visions. Lamentations just grieves. And in the wreckage the prophets start describing something bigger than a national comeback: a new agreement written on people's hearts, and a servant who suffers on behalf of others.

NebuchadnezzarDanielEzekielBabylon
Keep hold of

Losing everything is where the hope gets bigger, not smaller.

08538–430 BC

Coming home, sort of

Ezra · Nehemiah · Esther · Haggai · Zechariah · Malachi

Persia conquers Babylon and lets the exiles go home. They rebuild the temple — smaller, and the old men who remember the first one weep. Nehemiah rebuilds the city walls; Ezra re-teaches the law. Esther, meanwhile, saves the Jews who stayed in Persia, in a book that never once mentions God. Tobit and Judith sit alongside these \u2014 two smaller stories about keeping faith far from home.

But there's no king, they're still somebody's province, and the last prophet signs off promising that a messenger will come. Then the Old Testament simply stops, mid-sentence in narrative terms.

EzraNehemiahEstherSecond Temple
Keep hold of

The Old Testament ends unfinished on purpose. It's a story waiting for someone.

Sitting alongside all of the above

The books with no plot

Seven books don't advance the story — they're what people wrote while living it. If you open the Bible in the middle you'll probably land here, which is why it can feel like nothing is happening.

Psalms
150 songs and raw prayers — joy, rage, panic, thanks. The most-read book in the Bible.
Proverbs
Short, blunt lines of practical wisdom about money, work, sex, friendship and speech.
Job
A good man loses everything and demands to know why. God's answer is not the one anyone expects.
Ecclesiastes
A wealthy sceptic asks whether any of it means anything. Surprisingly modern.
Song of Songs
Unembarrassed love poetry between two lovers.
Wisdom
A defence of the life of faith, written in polished Greek for people embarrassed by their own tradition.
Sirach
A teacher's whole curriculum — money, friendship, temper, grief, table manners. The longest of the seven.
09430–5 BC

Between the testaments

1 & 2 Maccabees

Four centuries pass between Malachi and Matthew. Alexander the Great spreads Greek culture across the region; a Greek king later outlaws Jewish practice and desecrates the temple; a priest's family leads a revolt that wins, briefly, real independence. Then Rome arrives and installs a puppet king named Herod.

1 and 2 Maccabees tell that story, and they're the reason this stretch isn't a blank. Protestant Bibles leave them out, which is why the period is often called the four hundred silent years — but the events shaped everything. By the end, synagogues exist, religious parties like the Pharisees exist, and expectation is at fever pitch.

Keep hold of

When the New Testament opens, everyone is waiting for a messiah — and after the Maccabees, most of them expect him to arrive with an army.

The hinge

Everything before this points forward. Everything after it points back.

10c. 5 BC – AD 27

Jesus arrives

Matthew · Mark · Luke · John — the four gospels

Four books, one life, four angles. "Gospel" just means good news. They're not biographies in the modern sense; each writer selects and arranges to make a case to a different audience.

The facts they share: born to a young woman named Mary in an unimportant town, raised as a carpenter's son, unremarkable for thirty years. Then he's baptised by his relative John the Baptist, spends forty days alone in the desert, and starts speaking in public.

MaryJosephJohn the BaptistNazarethBethlehem
Keep hold of

Read one gospel first, not all four in a row. Mark is fastest and shortest. John is the most reflective. Luke is the most careful with detail.

11c. AD 27–30

Three years that split history

The middle of each gospel

He picks twelve unimpressive men to follow him, heals people, and eats with exactly the people religious society excluded — tax collectors, prostitutes, foreigners, the sick. He teaches mostly in short provocative stories called parables.

The message: God's kingdom is arriving right now, and it's upside down — the poor, the last and the enemies are in; the respectable and self-satisfied are struggling to get through the door. And he keeps saying and doing things — forgiving sins, claiming authority over the law — that sound to the religious leadership like a man claiming to be God.

The TwelvePeterParablesMiraclesSermon on the Mount
Keep hold of

The conflict isn't really about his ethics. It's about who he says he is.

12c. AD 30–33

The cross, and after

The last chapters of every gospel

He rides into Jerusalem at Passover to a crowd expecting a takeover. Within a week he's betrayed by one of his own, arrested after a final meal with them, tried overnight, and executed by crucifixion — Rome's public method for rebels and slaves. His followers scatter and hide.

Three days later they say the tomb is empty and that they've met him alive, eaten with him, touched him. Whatever a reader concludes about that claim, its effect is not in doubt: a terrified group becomes a movement that spreads across an empire and refuses to recant under execution.

JudasLast SupperPontius PilateCrucifixionResurrection
Keep hold of

This is the hinge of the entire book. Every page after it is written by people reacting to it.

13AD 33–62

The movement spreads

Acts

What the first followers did next. Peter preaches in Jerusalem and thousands join in a day. Persecution scatters them, which only spreads it further. The big argument of the early church gets settled: this isn't only for Jews.

Then Saul, a hardliner arresting Christians, has an encounter on the road to Damascus, changes sides completely, becomes Paul, and spends the rest of his life crossing the Roman empire starting churches — shipwrecks, riots, prisons and all. The book ends abruptly with him under house arrest in Rome.

PentecostPeterStephenPaulDamascus road
Keep hold of

Acts is the map for everything that follows. Read it before the letters and the letters stop feeling random.

14AD 48–95

Letters to real churches

Romans through Jude — 21 books

Most of the New Testament isn't story. It's mail. Paul and a handful of others writing to actual congregations and individuals with actual problems: factions, lawsuits, sex, money, food rules, grief, fraudulent teachers, and the pressure of being a tiny illegal minority.

Because they're answering specific questions, they contain the clearest explanations anywhere of what these people believed and how they thought it should change a life. Romans is the most systematic. James is the most practical. Philippians is the warmest.

PaulCorinthEphesusJamesPeterJohn
Keep hold of

Read them like someone else's mail you've been allowed to open. Always ask: what problem made them write this?

15c. AD 95

How it ends

Revelation

A vision given to John, exiled on a prison island, written in dense symbolic code — beasts, seals, trumpets, numbers, a woman, a dragon. It's the most argued-over book in the Bible and the one most often read badly.

Its first job was to tell churches under Roman pressure that the empire crushing them is not the thing that lasts. Read past the imagery to the final two chapters and you get the payoff of the whole library: God moves in with people permanently, death is finished, and the world is remade — a garden inside a city, with no exile at the gate.

JohnPatmosThe seven churchesNew Jerusalem
Keep hold of

It ends where it began — people and God in a garden, uninterrupted. That symmetry is the whole argument of the book in one shape.

Now actually read it

A first path through, in order

  1. MarkThe shortest gospel. About 90 minutes total. You meet Jesus before you meet anything else.
  2. ActsWhat his followers did next, and how a movement started. Reads like an adventure.
  3. Genesis 1–12Now go back to the start: the fracture, and the promise to Abraham.
  4. Exodus 1–20The rescue and the law — the story the rest of the Old Testament keeps referring to.
  5. 1 Maccabees 1–4The four centuries between the testaments, in the part that matters: the temple desecrated, and the revolt that took it back. This is the world Jesus was born into.
  6. Luke, then JohnSame life, more detail, more depth. By now you'll catch the Old Testament echoes — and know why the crowds wanted a fighter.
  7. Philippians, then RomansA short warm letter, then the big one that lays out the whole argument.
  8. Psalms, a few at a timeStart with 23, 51 and 139. Then wander.
Get a Bible with all 73 booksLook for the NRSV Catholic Edition, the NABRE, or the Jerusalem Bible. A standard NIV, ESV or NLT has only 66 and won't contain Tobit, Judith, Maccabees, Wisdom, Sirach or Baruch. The King James is beautiful and 400 years old — it's not the place to start.
Read in chunks, not versesA single verse pulled out of a chapter is the most common way to misread this book. Read whole chapters.
Ask three questions per passageWho was this written to? What did it mean to them? What's it saying about God or people?
Let the hard parts stay hardThere's violence, law code and genealogy in here. Mark it, move on, come back. Nobody understands all of it, including people who've read it for decades.

Also called the Torah or the Books of Moses. Origins, rescue and the founding of a nation, wrapped around the law code that defined it.

Wisdom Books

Books 22–28 · Job–Sirach

These don't move the story forward. They're what people wrote while living inside it — prayer, argument, doubt, advice and desire.

Why do good people suffer?

A blameless man loses his children, wealth and health. Three friends spend thirty chapters explaining why he must have deserved it, and they're wrong. God finally speaks and answers with questions rather than explanations.

Judgment first, then comfort.

Four accounts of one life, written for four audiences. Read one all the way through before comparing them.

Paul's Letters

Books 52–64 · Romans–Philemon

Real mail to real congregations with real problems, arranged roughly longest to shortest rather than in the order they were written.

Genesis 1–11

Adam & Eve

The first people, and the first mistake.

Placed in a garden with everything they need and one restriction, they take the one thing withheld and are put outside. Their names simply mean "human" and "life", which is a hint about how the story wants to be read.

The man who built the boat.

The founding family

Abraham

The one everything traces back to.

An elderly, childless man told to leave his country for a land he's never seen, on the promise of descendants, territory and blessing for the whole world. He believes it, doubts it, tries to arrange it himself, and is still the reference point for Jews, Christians and Muslims alike.

Abraham's wife, who laughed.

Childless into old age, she overhears a visitor say she'll have a son within the year and laughs out loud. The son is named Isaac, which means "he laughs".

The promised son, and the quietest patriarch.

Born to elderly parents, nearly sacrificed by his father in the most disturbing scene in Genesis, and then largely a bridge between two louder generations.

Jacob

The cheat who becomes Israel.

He swindles his brother out of an inheritance, runs, is swindled in turn by his uncle, and wrestles a stranger all night by a river before being renamed Israel. His twelve sons become the twelve tribes.

Joseph

Sold by his brothers, ends up running Egypt.

His father's favourite, thrown in a pit and sold into slavery, then imprisoned on a false accusation. He interprets Pharaoh's dreams, becomes second-in-command, and saves the region — and his brothers — from famine.

Same name, different person

The usual confusions

Three women called Mary

  • Mary of Nazareth — Jesus' mother.
  • Mary Magdalene — a follower he healed, and the first witness to the resurrection. Often wrongly assumed to be a prostitute; the text never says so.
  • Mary of Bethany — Martha's sister, who anoints his feet.

Two men called Saul

  • King Saul — Israel's first king, roughly 1,000 years before Jesus.
  • Saul of Tarsus — the persecutor who becomes the apostle Paul. Same name, no connection. Paul was the Roman version of his name, not a new one he was given.

Several men called James

  • James son of Zebedee — one of the twelve disciples, John's brother, killed early in Acts.
  • James the brother of Jesus — not a disciple during Jesus' life, later leads the Jerusalem church and writes the letter of James.

Several men called John

  • John the Baptist — the desert preacher, executed before Jesus.
  • John the disciple — one of the twelve, traditionally the gospel writer.
  • John of Patmos — the exile who wrote Revelation, often identified as the same man.

Several kings called Herod

  • Herod the Great — king at Jesus' birth; orders the killing of infants in Bethlehem.
  • Herod Antipas — his son; executes John the Baptist and questions Jesus at his trial.
  • Herod Agrippa — grandson; kills the apostle James and imprisons Peter in Acts.

Two Josephs, two Judahs

  • Joseph in Genesis — the one sold into Egypt. Joseph of Nazareth — Mary's husband, 1,700 years later.
  • Judah is Jacob's son and the name of the southern kingdom. Judas is the Greek form of the same name — which is why the betrayer and a perfectly ordinary tribe share it.

No one by that name here. Try another spelling, or clear the search.

Guide four · the vocabulary

Words nobody
stops to explain

Church language is a dialect, and most of it goes undefined because everyone assumes you already know. Here are the thirty-odd terms that do the heavy lifting, in plain English.

The load-bearing ideas

Learn these six first

Covenant

A binding agreement between two parties, with obligations on both sides — closer to a treaty or a marriage than a contract. The Bible's structure is a series of them: with Noah, Abraham, Moses, David, and finally the "new covenant" the New Testament claims Jesus establishes.

In plain termsA relationship with terms attached, meant to last.

Sin

Usually explained as breaking rules, which undersells it. The underlying image is missing a target or going off the road — living against the grain of how things were meant to work, and damaging yourself and others in the process. It describes a condition as much as individual acts.

In plain termsEverything that breaks the relationship — between people and God, and between people.

Grace

Something good given to someone who hasn't earned it and can't repay it. It's the word Christians use for the whole shape of what they think God does, and it's set deliberately against the idea of a scoreboard.

In plain termsA gift, not wages.

Faith

Not believing things without evidence, in the biblical sense. It's closer to trust — the sort you place in a person based on what you know of their character. The Hebrew and Greek words are about reliability and loyalty, not certainty.

In plain termsTrusting someone enough to act on it.

Repentance

The Greek word means changing your mind, and by extension turning around and walking the other way. It's about direction, not feeling sorry — regret can be part of it, but isn't the point.

In plain termsA U-turn.

Gospel

An old English word for good news — specifically the kind an announcer would proclaim about a battle won or a new king. It refers both to the message about Jesus and to the four books that tell his story.

In plain termsAn announcement, not advice.

Words about God and Jesus

Titles and claims

Messiah also: Christ

Hebrew for "anointed one" — someone marked out with oil for a role, usually a king. Christ is simply the Greek translation of the same word, so it's a title, not a surname. By Jesus' day it meant a promised king from David's line who would put things right.

In plain termsThe rightful king, long expected.

Lord

In the Old Testament, printed in small capitals, it stands in for God's personal name, which Jewish tradition avoids pronouncing. Applied to Jesus in the New Testament, it carries both the everyday sense of "master" and, deliberately, the heavier one.

In plain termsThe one in charge — with a loaded second meaning.

Holy

Set apart, other, not ordinary. It doesn't primarily mean morally spotless, though that follows; it means belonging to a different category, the way a wedding ring is different from other jewellery.

In plain termsSet apart for something.

Holy Spirit

God present and at work in people rather than observed from a distance. Occasional in the Old Testament, poured out widely from Acts chapter 2 onwards, and described as the source of guidance, courage and change in the New Testament letters.

In plain termsGod, up close and internal.

Trinity

The word never appears in the Bible. It's the term the church settled on for something the texts assume without explaining: that Father, Son and Spirit are each fully God, and that there is one God. Nobody claims it's tidy.

In plain termsOne God, three persons — the church's shorthand for a puzzle in the text.

Kingdom of God also: kingdom of heaven

Not a place you go when you die. It's the state of affairs where God's intentions are actually carried out — which Jesus announces as already breaking in and not yet complete. Most of his parables are describing what it's like.

In plain termsThings working the way they were meant to.

Words about what happens to people

The mechanics

Salvation also: saved

Rescue. In the Old Testament it's usually rescue from a concrete danger — slavery, an army, illness. In the New Testament it widens to rescue from sin and death. The question "are you saved?" is asking whether you're on the receiving end of that.

In plain termsBeing pulled out of something you couldn't get out of alone.

Righteous also: righteousness

Being in the right — both morally upright and, importantly, in good standing in a relationship. The New Testament argument is that people receive this standing rather than achieve it.

In plain termsBeing right with someone, not just being good.

Justification

A courtroom word: being declared not guilty and restored to good standing. Paul uses it constantly, and the Reformation was largely an argument about it.

In plain termsThe verdict comes back clear.

Redemption

A marketplace word: buying something back, or paying to free a slave or a prisoner. It carries a price, which is why it attaches to the crucifixion.

In plain termsBought back at a cost.

Atonement

Making amends so a broken relationship can be repaired — the coined English word literally reads "at-one-ment". The Old Testament sacrificial system exists for this; the New Testament argues Jesus' death does it once and for all.

In plain termsSettling the damage so the relationship can restart.

Mercy & Grace

Often paired and easily confused. Mercy is not getting the bad thing you had coming. Grace is getting the good thing you didn't.

In plain termsMercy withholds a penalty; grace hands over a gift.

Sanctification

The slow process of actually becoming what you've been declared to be. Long, uneven, and lifelong — the word most often used when someone asks why Christians still behave badly.

In plain termsGrowing into it, gradually.

Eternal life

In John's gospel, a quality of life that begins now rather than a duration that begins at death. It means life in full connection with God, which the writers assume death doesn't interrupt.

In plain termsA kind of life, not just a length of one.

Words about people and groups

Who's who by category

Prophet

Someone who speaks for God — mostly calling a society back to what it agreed to, and naming the injustice it has learned to ignore. Predicting the future is a minor part of the job.

In plain termsA whistleblower with a mandate.

Disciple & Apostle

A disciple is a student or apprentice, so all of Jesus' followers are disciples. An apostle is someone sent out with authority — the inner twelve, plus Paul and a few others.

In plain termsDisciple = learner. Apostle = one who's sent.

Pharisees & Sadducees

Two rival Jewish groups in Jesus' day. Pharisees were meticulous about the law and popular with ordinary people; Sadducees were the wealthy temple establishment who cooperated with Rome. Jesus argues with both, and "Pharisee" has since become an unfair byword for hypocrisy.

In plain termsThe strict popular movement, and the priestly establishment.

Gentile

Anyone who isn't Jewish. The word appears constantly in Acts and the letters because the central controversy of the early church was whether non-Jews could join without adopting Jewish law.

In plain termsEveryone else.

Church

The Greek word means an assembly of people called together. It never refers to a building in the New Testament — it's always the people, usually meeting in someone's house.

In plain termsA gathering, not a venue.

Satan also: the devil

The name means "the accuser" or "the adversary". The Bible says far less about him than popular culture assumes, and gives no systematic biography — he functions as the voice of accusation and the force working against what God is doing.

In plain termsThe opposition, and the prosecuting voice.

Words about practice

Things people do

Sabbath

One day in seven with no work, built into the law and grounded in both creation and the memory of slavery. The point is that people are not their output — a radical claim in a world of forced labour.

In plain termsA mandatory day off, with a reason behind it.

Passover

The annual Jewish meal remembering the escape from Egypt. Jesus' final meal with his followers was a Passover, which is why the imagery of lamb, blood and rescue runs through the accounts of his death.

In plain termsThe rescue anniversary meal.

Baptism

Going under water and coming up, as a public marker of joining. The image is washing and also burial and rebirth. Churches disagree about the age and the amount of water; nobody disagrees about the meaning.

In plain termsThe visible sign of starting.

Communion also: the Lord's Supper, Eucharist, Mass

Bread and wine shared in memory of Jesus' last meal and his death. Four names for one practice, and the differences between them are centuries of argument about what exactly is happening.

In plain termsThe shared meal at the centre of Christian worship.

Parable

A short story that hides its point in plain sight. Jesus' preferred teaching method — they invite you in, then turn and make a demand of you. They're not simple illustrations, and his audiences often left annoyed.

In plain termsA story with a hook in it.

Amen & Hallelujah

Two Hebrew words carried untranslated into most languages. Amen means "yes, truly, let it stand" — an agreement, not a full stop. Hallelujah means "praise the Lord".

In plain terms"Agreed" and "praise God".

Words about the book itself

How it got here

Testament

An older English word for covenant. So "Old Testament" and "New Testament" really mean the old agreement and the new one — the labels are a theological claim, not a filing system.

In plain termsAgreement.

Canon

The list of books recognised as scripture. Deuterocanonical also: the Apocrypha

The seven books in this site's Old Testament that Protestant Bibles leave out — Tobit, Judith, 1 and 2 Maccabees, Wisdom, Sirach and Baruch — plus additional passages in Esther and Daniel. Catholics call them deuterocanonical, meaning a second layer of the canon rather than a lesser one; Protestants call them the Apocrypha, meaning hidden or doubtful. Which word you use signals which tradition you're standing in.

Exegesis & Hermeneutics

Two words you'll meet in any serious study guide. Exegesis is working out what a passage meant to its original readers. Hermeneutics is the method you use to get from there to now.

A note on which Bible. This site follows the 73-book Catholic canon, in the order a Catholic Bible uses. Protestant Bibles contain 66 of these — they omit Tobit, Judith, 1 and 2 Maccabees, Wisdom, Sirach and Baruch, and use shorter versions of Esther and Daniel — and they order parts of the Old Testament differently. Orthodox Bibles include several books beyond the 73. All traditions share the same 27 New Testament books. The seven contested books are tagged Deuterocanon on the shelf, so you can always see which is which.
